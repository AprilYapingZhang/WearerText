\begin{abstract}
    Recent MLLMs have shown promise in scene-text-aware video understanding, yet their ability to understand scene text from the wearer's perspective in AI-glasses videos remains underexplored. AI-glasses videos are passively captured without a camera-frame preview, limiting the wearer's ability to adjust framing during recording. As a result, relevant text may be unstable, and the recorded view may not fully reflect the wearer's attention. The challenge is thus not merely to read visible text, but to identify and read the right text given the wearer's position, viewpoint trajectory, and goal.
To systematically evaluate this capability, we introduce \textbf{WearerText}, a benchmark for \textbf{Wearer}-Centered Scene \textbf{Text} Understanding, comprising 1{,}101 real-world AI-glasses videos and 15{,}336 QA pairs spanning 13 tasks.
These tasks are organized into a three-level diagnostic hierarchy: \textit{L1 Text Perception}, \textit{L2 Contextual Understanding}, and \textit{L3 Wearer-Grounded Reasoning}, targeting unstable text perception, wearer-view alignment, and goal-oriented text reasoning, respectively.
To ensure reliable construction and evaluation, we develop \textsc{MAH-V}, a multi-agent hybrid verification pipeline for QA construction, and propose VerEval, a Verified-evidence metric for open-ended Evaluation.
Benchmarking 20 recent MLLMs shows that current models still struggle with wearer-centered scene text understanding, particularly on tasks requiring wearer-view alignment and goal-oriented text reasoning.\footnote{https://github.com/AprilYapingZhang/WearerText}   
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Visual Text Understanding.}
Visual text understanding has evolved from robust scene text recognition and VQA~\cite{zhang2021robust,8953586,biten2019scene} to layout-aware document analysis, image translation, and multimodal benchmarks across multilingual, scientific, and wearable settings~\cite{zhang2026promptdla,zhang2025reading,liang2025ssr,zhang2025icdardimt,li2026mmtit,zhang2025hiscibench,chang2025wearvqa}.
Among wearable benchmarks, WearVQA~\cite{chang2025wearvqa} is the most closely related, but focuses on general VQA over single wearable images.
In contrast, \dataset~targets scene text understanding in dynamic AI-glasses videos, where models must handle unstable text, align textual evidence with the wearer's view, and reason according to the wearer's goal.

\noindent\textbf{Egocentric Video Understanding.}
Egocentric benchmarks study visual understanding from a first-person perspective and are closely related to wearable assistance.
WearVQA~\cite{chang2025wearvqa} evaluates wearable-image understanding, while EgoTextVQA~\cite{zhou2025egotextvqa} extends text-aware VQA to head-mounted videos from driving and indoor activities.
However, existing egocentric benchmarks do not explicitly decompose scene text understanding into unstable text perception, wearer-view alignment, and goal-oriented reasoning over changing viewpoints.
\dataset~focuses on this wearer-centered setting, where the relevance of textual evidence depends on the wearer's position, viewpoint trajectory, and goal.

\noindent\textbf{Text-Centric Video Understanding.}
Several benchmarks evaluate text-aware video understanding~\cite{zhao2022towards,wu2021bilingual,reddy2020roadtext,zhou2025egotextvqa,wu2023icdar}.
RoadTextVQA~\cite{reddy2020roadtext} focuses on driving scenes from vehicle-mounted cameras, while M4-ViTeVQA~\cite{zhao2022towards} covers diverse YouTube videos.
More recent benchmarks, including MME-VideoOCR~\cite{shi2025mmevideoocr} and VidText~\cite{yang2025vidtext}, further evaluate OCR-related capabilities in videos.
These benchmarks primarily assess text recognition and understanding from the recorded video itself, but do not systematically evaluate whether models can identify text relevant to the wearer as the viewpoint changes.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes these differences.
Unlike prior text-aware and egocentric benchmarks, \dataset~is built from real-world AI-glasses videos and organizes evaluation into a three-level diagnostic hierarchy.

\subsection{Main Results}




\noindent\textbf{Alignment Between VerEval and Human Judgment.}
We evaluate VerEval on 390 QA pairs annotated by three experts and finalized by majority vote as \textit{Correct} or \textit{Wrong}. We compare against SAS, a transformer-based semantic answer similarity metric~\citep{risch2021semantic}, and ANLS, a normalized edit-similarity metric widely used in scene-text VQA~\citep{biten2019scene}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:score_distribution}, our VerEval Score aligns more closely with human judgments. It yields a larger Correct--Wrong mean gap (0.402 vs. 0.112/0.094). These results indicate that VerEval provides a more reliable evaluation signal than ANLS and SAS for open-ended wearer-centered scene text understanding. We therefore report the main leaderboard with VerEval scores in Table~\ref{tab:final_overall_results}, while detailed ANLS and SAS results are provided in Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:anls_results} and~\ref{tab:sas_results}. VerEval produces scores on the raw [0,1] scale; we multiply them by 100 when reporting results for readability in Tables.

\subsection{Key Findings}
We conduct extensive experiments to thoroughly analyze the challenges presented by \dataset.  Our analysis centers on the following questions: 
\noindent\textbf{Q1: How do tasks differ in difficulty?}
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:model_size_scaling}, across both Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL families, task difficulty increases from L1 to L3. L1 perception scores highest, L2 understanding trails behind, and L3 reasoning remains most challenging. This clear separation ($L1 > L2 > L3$) confirms that task complexity grows with each level. Notably, the substantial performance gap between L1 and L3 (up to 20 points) suggests that current MLLMs struggle to move from reading unstable text to wearer-view alignment and goal-oriented text evidence selection.




\noindent\textbf{Q2: Why do current models fail?}
To move beyond aggregate scores, we manually categorize GPT-5.2's incorrect predictions on the held-out test set. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:faifurecases}(d), the dominant failure type is {goal-oriented text evidence reasoning} (57.4\%), followed by {unstable text recognition} (26.6\%) and {wearer-view alignment} (16.0\%). These results suggest that the key bottleneck is not only OCR, but the ability to select and reason over the right textual evidence under the wearer's view. Figure~\ref{fig:faifurecases} illustrates these failure modes. In (a), the model fails to recognize briefly visible scrolling text on a digital screen, showing the difficulty of unstable text reading under blur and short exposure. In (b), the model incorrectly includes years from visually similar awards, indicating weak temporal evidence accumulation and fine-grained text selection. In (c), the model gives an incorrect navigation suggestion because it fails to align visible shop signs with the wearer's spatial context. Overall, these cases show that AI-glasses text understanding requires the joint ability to read unstable text, ground it from the wearer's viewpoint, and accumulate goal-relevant evidence over time. 

\noindent\textbf{Q3: Does the benchmark require full AI-glasses video?}
We compare GPT-5.2 with full video, sampled keyframes, and layout-aware OCR text. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostic_bars}(a), full video consistently outperforms reduced inputs across L1--L3 and overall. The gap is modest for image-only input but much larger for OCR-only input, especially at L3, indicating that higher-level tasks require visual context and temporally ordered wearer-view signals beyond extracted text.



\noindent\textbf{Q4: Does memory help AI-glasses text understanding?}
We compare four memory strategies on GPT-5.2 in Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostic_bars}(b): no memory, unstructured multimodal memory with raw past frames, OCR-derived text memory with concatenated OCR tokens, and structured text memory with distilled per-frame text indexed by the wearer's viewpoint trajectory and paired with a reasoning prompt. Three task-specific patterns emerge.
(i)~\textbf{Memory helps when evidence is temporal.} Gains concentrate on L3 \textit{Wearer-Grounded Reasoning} (e.g., L3.1 HII and L3.5 GOC), while frame-local L1 perception is largely unaffected. This suggests that the L3 deficit in Q1 partly reflects a memory-architecture limitation rather than model capacity alone.
(ii)~\textbf{Structure beats raw recall.} OCR-only memory brings little gain, whereas trajectory-indexed distilled text with a reasoning prompt improves overall performance by $+2.0$. It is also the only configuration that helps L3.4 STN and L3.6 VTD, both of which explicitly query the wearer's movement.
(iii)~\textbf{Raw multimodal memory hurts.} Stale frames degrade accuracy below the no-memory baseline, suggesting that re-perception competes with current-frame perception and amplifies the unstable-text errors.
\emph{Design implication:} future systems should maintain a low-bandwidth episodic record of distilled text indexed by the viewpoint trajectory rather than a verbatim multimodal buffer.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduce \dataset, a benchmark for evaluating AI-glasses text understanding in real-world wearable scenarios. Through our three-level task hierarchy, we assess {20} recent MLLMs on real-world public text-use challenges. Our findings have shown that current models remain limited on wearer-centered scene text understanding and reasoning, especially when answers require selecting relevant text evidence from partial observations according to the wearer's goal. These results suggest that progress in this setting requires stronger wearer-view alignment, structured memory over observed text, and intent-aware reasoning.

\section*{Limitations}

\dataset~ is collected from real wearable-glasses videos, but its coverage is still bounded by the recording locations, participant behaviors, device characteristics, and public-space scenarios included in our study. Although the benchmark contains multilingual and mixed-language text, its distribution is not intended to represent all languages, regions, signage conventions, or accessibility needs. Future extensions should include broader geographic coverage, more diverse users, and additional low-resource languages.

Our task design focuses on offline question answering over short egocentric clips. Thus, ``goal-oriented text evidence selection'' refers to selecting relevant textual evidence within 5--10 second interaction primitives according to the wearer's current goal, rather than long-horizon memory over minutes or hours. This setting enables controlled comparison across models, but it does not fully capture interactive use cases in which a wearable assistant can ask clarification questions, adapt its guidance, or operate under real-time latency and energy constraints.

\dataset~ is wearer-centered in the sense that questions are grounded in the camera-view trajectory of the person wearing the device. However, our RayNeo recordings do not include eye-tracking signals, so camera view should not be interpreted as true gaze. Future versions with synchronized gaze sensing could separate head direction, eye fixation, and user intention more precisely.
